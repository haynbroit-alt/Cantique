# Analogy test: Bitcoin 2009-2012 entrepreneurial opportunities vs the quantum ecosystem (Nov 2026)

Scope: the historical side (Bitcoin 2009-2012, with 2013 only as aftermath) and the structural comparison with quantum as of November 2026. Research date: 22 Nov 2026. Method note: Wikipedia and Bitcoin Wiki pages were read as raw wikitext on 22 Nov 2026. Some Bitcoin Wiki pages look recently rewritten, so treat them as secondary sources. The two Moore & Christin exchange-risk papers were read in full. The McKinsey 2026, QED-C 2026 and BCG 2026 quantum reports were read from text copies already in the shared scratchpad. Survivorship bias is flagged throughout: almost everything below describes visible businesses, and invisible failures (small merchants, faucets, pools, scams that left no trace) are not counted.

## 1. Catalogue of Bitcoin 2009-2012 opportunities: who, when, how much capital, what happened

### Takeaway
Between 2010 and 2012 almost every infrastructure role appeared. Exchanges, custodial wallets, block explorers, light wallets, payment processors, mining pools, ASIC makers, media, a standards foundation, gambling and an illicit market all launched, most of them as software side projects with no outside capital, or with seed rounds of $150k-$510k. Their outcomes were bimodal. Most of the 2010 first movers died (Bitcoin Market, Mt. Gox, MyBitcoin, Bitcoinica, TradeHill, Bitfloor, BitInstant). A handful of mid-2011/2012 entrants became lasting infrastructure (Coinbase, Kraken, Bitstamp, BitPay, Blockchain.com), and they did so mainly after 2013, with venture capital and licences.

### Cited Findings

**Price context (the "asset" side of every business)**
- Jan 2009 to Mar 2010: bitcoin was worth "basically nothing", with no exchange or market. In March 2010 a user auctioned 10,000 BTC for $50 in total. On 22 May 2010, 10,000 BTC (about $41) bought two pizzas in the "first notable retail transaction". Parity with $1 came in Feb-Apr 2011. Bitcoin reached $1,242 on 29 Nov 2013. — [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)
- The 2011 bubble peaked at $31.91 on 8 June 2011 (Bitcoin Wiki; Fortune gives $29.58 on 9 June). On 19 June 2011 a compromised Mt. Gox admin account crashed the price on Mt. Gox from about $17.50 to $0.01, and bitcoin fell to about $2 by November 2011 (Fortune: $2.14 on 18 Nov 2011), a drop of more than 90% from the peak. — [Bitcoin Wiki, 2011](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/2011); [BitMEX Research](https://blog.bitmex.com/the-june-2011-flash-crash-to-0-01); [Fortune, 2 Jan 2021](https://dc.fortune.com/2021/01/02/is-bitcoin-a-bubble-will-it-crash-again-history/); [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/a-dazzling-history-of-bitcoins-ups-and-downs)

**Exchanges**
- *Bitcoin Market* (operator: bitcointalk user "dwdollar"). Trading began on 17 March 2010, making it the earliest exchange in these sources. It dropped PayPal on 4 June 2011 because of fraud. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Bitcoin Market](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoin_Market). Moore & Christin list it as active 4/2010-6/2011, closed and breached. — [Moore & Christin 2013, Table 1](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf)
- *Mt. Gox*. Jed McCaleb had bought mtgox.com in Jan 2007 for a "Magic: The Gathering Online" card-trading site that he abandoned. In July 2010 he read about bitcoin on Slashdot and repurposed the domain as an exchange. In March 2011 he sold it to Mark Karpelès, writing that it "would require more time than I have right now". It was hacked in mid-June 2011. By April 2013 it handled more than 70% of the world's bitcoin trades. On 15 May 2013 US authorities seized its Dwolla-linked accounts because it had not registered as a money transmitter with FinCEN. It filed for bankruptcy on 28 Feb 2014 with 850,000 BTC missing (later revised to 650,000 after 200,000 were "found"). A leaked document cited 744,408 BTC lost "in a theft which went undetected for years". There were about 127,000 creditors. The collapse led Japan to create its first formal exchange regulation. — [Wikipedia, Mt. Gox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Gox). Mt. Gox averaged more than 40,000 BTC a day; median exchange volume was much lower. — [Moore & Christin 2013](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf)
- *Bitstamp*. Founded in August 2011 in Slovenia by Nejc Kodrič and Damijan Merlak "as a European-focused alternative to then-dominant" Mt. Gox. It moved its registration to the UK in April 2013 "due to the lack of adequate financial and legal services in Slovenia", and to Luxembourg in 2016. It received an EU payment-institution licence in April 2016. A Belgian investment company bought it in 2018. Robinhood completed its acquisition on 2 June 2025 for about $200M cash; at the time Bitstamp held more than 50 licences and had more than 500k funded retail and about 5,000 institutional customers. — [Wikipedia, Bitstamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitstamp). It was hacked in January 2015 for about 19,000 BTC (about $5M). — [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)
- *Kraken (Payward)*. Co-founded in July 2011 in San Francisco by Jesse Powell, Thanh Luu and Michael Gronager. Powell had consulted for Mt. Gox in 2011 and "began working on an alternative exchange with security controls as its foundation". Kraken launched publicly only in September 2013. It received a Wyoming SPDI bank charter in September 2020 (the first held by a US crypto exchange). It sought funding at a valuation above $20B in 2021, had $207B in quarterly volume by 2025, and was reported in March 2025 to be planning an IPO in early 2026. Kraken also helped the Mt. Gox trustee process claims. — [Wikipedia, Kraken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraken_(cryptocurrency_exchange)); [Wikipedia, Mt. Gox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Gox)
- *BTC-e*. Founded in July 2011 by Alexander Vinnik and Aleksandr Bilyuchenko, and handled about 3% of global volume in Feb 2015. The US DOJ shut it on 26 July 2017 with a 21-count money-laundering indictment that included laundering Mt. Gox hack proceeds. — [Wikipedia, BTC-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BTC-e)
- *Coinbase*. Founded in June 2012 by Brian Armstrong (ex-Airbnb engineer), who received $150,000 from Y Combinator. Fred Ehrsam (ex-Goldman Sachs) joined after seeing Armstrong's Reddit posts. Ben Reeves of Blockchain.info was meant to be a co-founder but split with Armstrong over custody: Reeves wanted users to control their keys, while Armstrong wanted the platform to hold them. Coinbase launched buy/sell by bank transfer in Oct 2012 and was selling more than $1M of BTC a month by Feb 2013. It raised a $5M Series A in May 2013 (Union Square Ventures), $25M in Dec 2013 (a16z, USV, Ribbit) and $75M in Jan 2015. It went public by direct listing on Nasdaq on 14 April 2021 at a $250 reference price (about $47B), after a quarter with $1.8B in revenue, and is now an S&P 500 member. — [Wikipedia, Coinbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinbase); [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin); [Wikipedia, Blockchain.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockchain.com)
- *Bitcoin.de* (Germany): active from 8/2011 and still operating in early 2013, with about 1,204 BTC/day. — [Moore & Christin 2013, Table 1](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf). Its founders, capital and current status were not verified in this pass.
- *TradeHill* (US). Went live 8 June 2011 and was the second-largest exchange at its peak. It had Dwolla payment anomalies in July 2011 and announced its shutdown on 13 Feb 2012. — [Bitcoin Wiki, TradeHill](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/TradeHill). Moore & Christin record it as breached, with customers repaid. — [Moore & Christin 2013](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf)
- *Intersango* (UK). Launched 6 July 2011 on open-source exchange software. It closed its BTC/USD market in Oct 2012 and its BTC/GBP market on 19 Dec 2012 "after being unable to re-establish a UK banking relationship". — [Bitcoin Wiki, Intersango](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Intersango)
- *Bitfloor* (New York). Operated from 2011 and registered with FinCEN as a Money Service Business on 15 March 2012. About 24,000 BTC were stolen on 4 Sept 2012, and it halted on 17 April 2013 after its bank closed its account. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Bitfloor](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitfloor)
- *BitInstant* (NYC, OTC cash on-ramp). Announced 23 Aug 2011 by Gareth Nelson and Charlie Shrem, it offered retail cash deposits and Dwolla funding into Mt. Gox, Bitstamp and BTC-e. By May 2013 it processed about 30% of the money flowing into and out of bitcoin. It ceased around July 2013, and Shrem, its CEO and compliance officer, pleaded guilty in 2014 in a Silk Road-related case. — [Bitcoin Wiki, BitInstant](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/BitInstant); [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)

**Wallets, explorers, trading services**
- *Blockchain.info / Blockchain.com*. Established in 2011 by Ben Reeves as "the first Bitcoin blockchain explorer". It later built a wallet that "accounted for 28% of bitcoin transactions between 2012 and 2020" and raised $190M in VC between 2012 and Feb 2021. — [Wikipedia, Blockchain.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockchain.com)
- *Electrum* (thin client). Announced and released on 5 Nov 2011, and still maintained (v4.x). — [Bitcoin Wiki, Electrum](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Electrum)
- *MyBitcoin* (eWallet). Its operators were anonymous and it claimed to be an LLC in Nevis. It went offline on 29 July 2011, then said 51% of its holdings had been stolen and that it would return 49% of balances. — [Bitcoin Wiki, MyBitcoin](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/MyBitcoin)
- *Bitcoinica* (leveraged CFD trading). Launched on the forum on 8 Sept 2011 by "zhoutong", it kept its funds at Mt. Gox and in bank accounts. It lost 43,554 BTC in the March 2012 Linode compromise, went offline in May 2012 after a second incident, and a New Zealand receiver was announced in Aug 2012. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Bitcoinica](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoinica); [Moore & Christin 2013](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf)

**Payments and merchant adoption**
- *BitPay*. Founded in May 2011 by Tony Gallippi and Stephen Pair to provide checkout for merchants. It had more than 1,000 merchants by Oct 2012. It raised a $510,000 seed and moved from Orlando to Atlanta in Jan 2013, then raised $2M from Founders Fund in May 2013. It had 10,000 merchants by Sept 2013 and 12,000 by Jan 2014, and had processed more than $34M (270,830 BTC) by 2013. — [Wikipedia, BitPay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BitPay); [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)
- Merchant and donation adoption was marginal and reversible. The EFF accepted BTC in Jan 2011 and stopped in June 2011 "citing concerns about a lack of legal precedent". WikiLeaks took BTC donations from June 2011. WordPress accepted BTC from Nov 2012. — [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)

**Mining**
- *Slush Pool* (Bitcoin.cz). Announced on BitcoinTalk on 27 Nov 2010 as "the first publicly known bitcoin mining pool" and still operating as Braiins Pool since its rename in Sept 2022. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Slush Pool](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Slush_Pool)
- *Butterfly Labs* (Kansas City). Founded in June 2012 by Nasser Ghoseiri and Sonny Vleisides to sell ASIC miners on pre-order. Machines were delivered late or never. The FTC sued in 2014, final orders came on 18 Feb 2016, and the company "effectively ceased". — [Bitcoin Wiki, Butterfly Labs](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Butterfly_Labs)
- *Avalon*. Among the first commercial ASICs (the 2012-2013 bitcointalk "Avalon Project"). It consolidated into Canaan, which listed on Nasdaq in 2019. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Avalon](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Avalon)
- *Bitmain*. Founded in 2013 by Micree Zhan and Jihan Wu. Its first product, the Antminer S1, delivered 180 GH/s. — [Wikipedia, Bitmain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitmain)

**Media, community, standards**
- *Bitcoin Magazine*. Its founders organised in Dec 2011 as "BitTalk Magazine". Web content started in March 2012 and the first print issue appeared on 1 May 2012. Co-founders included Vitalik Buterin and Mihai Alisie (then in Romania). It is now owned by BTC Inc (Nashville). — [Bitcoin Wiki, Bitcoin Magazine](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoin_Magazine); [Wikipedia, Bitcoin Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitcoin_Magazine)
- *Bitcoin Weekly*, a community magazine published by forum user "Kiba". — [Bitcoin Wiki, Bitcoin Weekly](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoin_Weekly)
- *Bitcointalk* was the distribution channel for launches: the pizza offer, Slush Pool, Bitcoinica and Electrum were all announced there. — [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin); [Bitcoin Wiki, Slush Pool](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Slush_Pool); [Bitcoin Wiki, Bitcoinica](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoinica); [Bitcoin Wiki, Electrum](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Electrum)
- *Bitcoin Foundation*. Launched in Sept 2012 to promote "standardization, protection, and promotion of the open source protocol". Peter Vessenes was founding chair and Gavin Andresen chief scientist. Vice-chair Charlie Shrem was arrested in Jan 2014 and Karpelès resigned in Feb 2014. It was revealed to be "nearly insolvent" in July 2015. — [Wikipedia, Bitcoin Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitcoin_Foundation); [Forbes, Matonis, 27 Sept 2012](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonmatonis/2012/09/27/bitcoin-foundation-launches-to-drive-bitcoins-advancement/)

**Gambling, investors, illicit context**
- *SatoshiDice* (Erik Voorhees, launched 2012). An on-chain dice game criticised for spamming the blockchain. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Satoshi Dice](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Satoshi_Dice). Between Aug 2012 and Feb 2013 it raised 50,600 BTC by selling 13M shares for bitcoin. It bought them back in July 2013 for 45,500 BTC and was sold the same month for 126,315 BTC (about $11.5M; PBS says $12.4M). The SEC charged Voorhees in June 2014 with selling unregistered shares, and he settled for about $51k. — [SEC press release 2014-111](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2014-111); [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/bitcoin-gambling-sites-fly-regulatory-radar); [The Next Web, 19 Jul 2013](https://thenextweb.com/news/first-major-bitcoin-acquisition-sees-gambling-site-satoshidice-sold-for-11-5-million); [TechCrunch, 3 Jun 2014](https://techcrunch.com/2014/06/03/bitcoin-entrepreneur-pays-51k-to-settle-with-the-sec-after-selling-shares-illegally)
- *Barry Silbert* (SecondMarket founder, 2004) "began buying up Bitcoin in 2012". He sold SecondMarket to Nasdaq and founded Digital Currency Group in Oct 2015 with Genesis and Grayscale as subsidiaries. — [Wikipedia, Barry Silbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Silbert)
- *Silk Road* (Ross Ulbricht) launched in Feb 2011 and became widely known after a Gawker article in June 2011. It facilitated sales of 9,519,664 BTC between Feb 2011 and July 2013. The FBI shut it in Oct 2013 and seized about 26,000 BTC. — [Wikipedia, Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road_(marketplace)); [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)

### Inferences
- **Capital needed.** For the exchange, wallet, explorer, pool, dice and media roles, it was effectively zero to low six figures. Mt. Gox was a repurposed hobby domain, sold after about 8 months. Coinbase started with $150k from YC. BitPay ran about 20 months before a $510k seed. The only capital-intensive role was ASIC manufacturing (2012-2013), and its early entrants (Butterfly Labs) were pre-order businesses that ended in FTC action.
- **Technical barrier.** Competent web or software engineering. No credential, licence or lab was required. The scarce skill was operational security, and nearly every first-generation failure was a security or custody failure.
- **Time to revenue.** Weeks to months. Fees on exchange volume, dice house edge and merchant fees were immediate. BitPay reached 1,000 merchants in about 17 months. Coinbase reached $1M a month in sales about 4 months after launching its buy/sell service. Revenue scale stayed small until the 2013 price rise. The businesses' own BTC treasuries appreciated too: SatoshiDice's exit was priced in BTC.
- **Roles summary.** Retail exchange (2010-2011), custodial e-wallet (2011), block explorer and data (2011), light wallet (2011), merchant processor (2011), cash on-ramp/OTC (2011), mining pool (2010), ASIC (2012-2013), print and web media (2012), standards foundation (2012), on-chain gambling (2012), illicit marketplace (2011), and early asset accumulation by investors (2012).

### Gaps
- Not verified with sources in this pass: the GPU mining transition (2010), Gavin Andresen's 2010 faucet (5 BTC per visitor), the "Bitcoin Opportunity Fund" (2012/2013: structure, size, first investments), the Winklevoss twins' 2012-2013 purchases (reported as about 1% of supply), Electrum's founder attribution, Bitcoin.de's founders and capital, and the founder ages of Bitcoinica and Bitcoin Magazine.
- Mt. Gox civil-rehabilitation repayment progress in 2024-2026 was not checked; the extracted Wikipedia sentences stop at a March 2018 trustee statement.
- Kraken's IPO status as of Nov 2026 is unverified (a March 2025 report said early 2026).
- Popper's *Digital Gold* and founder interviews were not consulted directly.

## 2. Base rates and survivorship: how risky was "being early"?

### Takeaway
"Being early" in Bitcoin was mostly a way to fail. 45% of the exchanges set up before 2013 had closed by early 2013 (median lifetime 381 days), and about half of the 80 founded in 2010-2015 had closed by 2015. Breaches were common and multiplied the odds of closure by 13 in the quarter they occurred. Customers often lost funds. Of the 2010-2012 cohort, only a few firms became indispensable, and they shared traits: they entered after the first wave, put security and compliance first, held banking relationships and licences, raised VC, and built liquidity or distribution network effects.

### Cited Findings
- Moore & Christin studied 40 exchanges established over roughly 2010 to early 2013, and 18 had closed (45%), "with customer account balances often wiped out". The median lifetime was 381 days. Nine exchanges suffered breaches and five of those closed. Of the 18 that closed, evidence on reimbursement existed for 11: five did not reimburse and six claimed they did. In a Cox model, doubling daily volume cut the hazard of closure by 16%. Breach had a positive but non-significant effect (p = 0.13), and the jurisdiction's AML/CFT score was not predictive (p = 0.92). Popular exchanges were more likely to be breached. — [Moore & Christin, "Beware the Middleman", FC 2013](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf)
- Closed exchanges in that sample, with active dates: BitcoinMarket (4/10-6/11), Bitomat (4/11-8/11), FreshBTC, Bitcoin7, ExchangeBitCoins.com, Bitchange.pl, Brasil Bitcoin Market, Aqoin, Global Bitcoin Exchange, Bitcoin2Cash, TradeHill (6/11-2/12), World Bitcoin Exchange, Ruxum, btctree, btcex.com, IMCEX, Crypto X Change, Bitmarket.eu. Mt. Gox, btc-e, Bitfloor and Vircurex were breached but still open in early 2013. — [Moore & Christin 2013, Table 1](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf)
- The follow-up study covered 80 exchanges established 2010-2015, and 38 had closed. 25 suffered breaches and 15 of those closed. A breach was associated with 13 times higher odds of closing in the same quarter. Each doubling of volume cut the odds of closure by about 12%. Exchanges serving niche fiat currencies with at most one competitor were less likely to close. In 15 of 20 cases with archive data, two-factor authentication was adopted before the breach, so security measures were not a reaction to breaches. — [Moore, Christin & Szurdi, ACM TOIT 2018](https://jszurdi.github.io/content/academicpapers/toit18.pdf)
- The same authors note that "most of the risk Bitcoin holders face stems from interacting with these intermediaries, which operate as de facto centralized authorities". — [Moore, Christin & Szurdi 2018](https://jszurdi.github.io/content/academicpapers/toit18.pdf)
- Later fates of firms that were still open in early 2013: Mt. Gox went bankrupt in Feb 2014 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Gox)). Bitfloor halted in April 2013 when its bank closed its account ([Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitfloor)). BTC-e was seized by the DOJ in July 2017 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BTC-e)). Intersango lost its UK bank in Dec 2012 ([Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Intersango)). Bitstamp survived and was sold to Robinhood for about $200M in June 2025 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitstamp)).
- Other failure and enforcement modes in the window: custodial theft with anonymous operators (MyBitcoin, July 2011, [Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/MyBitcoin)); hosting compromise of a leveraged platform (Bitcoinica, 43,554 BTC, March 2012, [Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoinica)); FTC action against ASIC pre-sales (Butterfly Labs, 2014-2016, [Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Butterfly_Labs)); SEC action over shares sold for BTC (SatoshiDice, 2014, [SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2014-111)); criminal AML charges against an on-ramp founder (BitInstant/Shrem, [Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/BitInstant)); seizure of the dominant exchange's US accounts for not being a registered money transmitter (Mt. Gox, May 2013, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)); and near-insolvency of the standards body (Bitcoin Foundation, 2015, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitcoin_Foundation)).
- Survivors that became infrastructure:
  - Coinbase: founded June 2012, YC then VC, custodial by design, bank-transfer rails, listed in 2021 at about $47B. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinbase)
  - Kraken: founded 2011 but launched Sept 2013 "with security controls as its foundation", bank charter in 2020. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraken_(cryptocurrency_exchange))
  - Bitstamp: moved to the UK for banking and legal services, EU licence in 2016, more than 50 licences by 2025. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitstamp)
  - BitPay: merchant network grew from 1,000 to 12,000 between Oct 2012 and Jan 2014, with VC from Founders Fund. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BitPay)
  - Blockchain.com: the explorer data position became a wallet behind 28% of transactions, with $190M of VC. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockchain.com)

### Inferences
- **Base rate.** About 45-50% of visible early exchanges failed within roughly 1-5 years. Out of the roughly 40 exchanges active by early 2013, only about one (Bitstamp), and possibly two or three (Bitcoin.de and Mercado Bitcoin, unverified), are still independent or acquired brands of significance in 2026. That is a long-run success rate of roughly 2.5-7.5%. Mt. Gox and btc-e, the volume leaders, both ended catastrophically. This is a lower bound on failure, because the sample covers only exchanges visible on bitcoincharts, with unknown attrition among smaller services.
- **Survivorship bias, explicit.** Coinbase and Kraken, the two biggest 2026 winners, are not in the 2013 sample at all: Kraken had not yet launched, and Coinbase was a broker rather than an exchange. Retrospectives that cite "Coinbase, Kraken, Bitstamp, BitPay, Blockchain.com" as proof that being early paid off are selecting the few right-tail outcomes from a cohort where failure was the modal outcome.
- **Common traits of survivors.**
  - Timing: they entered as the second wave (mid-2011 to 2012, or a 2013 launch), after watching the first wave's security failures.
  - Security and custody discipline treated as the product.
  - Regulatory positioning: FinCEN registration, licences, banking relationships.
  - Raising VC in 2013-2015, once institutional capital arrived.
  - Network effects in liquidity (exchanges) or distribution (merchant networks, wallet user bases).
  - First-mover status alone was not one of the traits; the first movers are almost all dead.
- **Banking access** was the most frequent non-hack killer: Intersango, Bitfloor, and Bitstamp's move to the UK. Regulation hit later (2013-2017) and was largely about AML/money transmission, not prohibition.
- **Exit asymmetry within the survivors.** Bitstamp, a top-3 exchange for years, exited after about 14 years for about $200M, while Coinbase listed at about $47B. Even among survivors the payoff was a steep power law.

### Gaps
- No systematic base rate was found for non-exchange businesses (wallets, pools, merchant processors, media, faucets) in 2009-2012.
- The current status of Vircurex, Bitcoin-24, CampBX, BTC China, Mercado Bitcoin, Bitcoin.de, Intersango (final closure) and VirWoX was not verified.
- Total customer losses aggregated across 2010-2012 failures were not compiled.

## 3. Enabling conditions: why near-zero-capital individuals could build these businesses

### Takeaway
Seven conditions combined.
- An open, permissionless protocol anyone could build on without asking.
- A native asset that was simultaneously the product, the payment rail, the capital market and the founders' treasury, and that appreciated by about 5 orders of magnitude from May 2010 to Nov 2013.
- No specific regulation until about 2013.
- A single global forum that served as distribution.
- Consumer-scale (often illicit) demand.
- Commodity hardware for mining until the 2013 ASIC era.
- Software-only products.

The binding constraints were security skill and banking access, not capital or credentials.

### Cited Findings
- Permissionless and software-only: a hobby domain could be turned into the world's main exchange (Mt. Gox, July 2010). — [Wikipedia, Mt. Gox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Gox). Exchanges ran on open-source exchange code (Intersango's software was announced on 17 March 2011). — [Bitcoin Wiki, Intersango](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Intersango)
- The native asset was also a capital market: SatoshiDice raised 50,600 BTC by selling 13M shares for bitcoin, without registration, from Aug 2012 to Feb 2013. — [SEC press release 2014-111](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2014-111). The asset's appreciation also inflated business exits (SatoshiDice sold for 126,315 BTC, about $11.5M, in July 2013). — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/first-major-bitcoin-acquisition-sees-gambling-site-satoshidice-sold-for-11-5-million)
- Appreciation magnitude: 10,000 BTC was worth about $41 (May 2010) and 1 BTC was worth $1,242 (Nov 2013), a rise of about 300,000x. — [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)
- There was initially no regulation and no observable regulatory effect. In 2013 the AML/CFT score of an exchange's jurisdiction did not predict closure (p = 0.92). The authors suggest that "regulatory oversight is not triggering closures". — [Moore & Christin 2013](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/nicolasc/publications/MC-FC13.pdf). Enforcement began in 2013 (the Mt. Gox Dwolla seizure for operating as an unregistered money transmitter). — [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)
- A single global community and distribution channel: launches were announced on Bitcointalk (Slush Pool on 27 Nov 2010, Bitcoinica on 8 Sept 2011, Electrum on 5 Nov 2011), and so was the pizza trade. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Slush Pool](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Slush_Pool); [Bitcoin Wiki, Bitcoinica](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoinica); [Bitcoin Wiki, Electrum](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Electrum); [Wikipedia, History of bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)
- Consumer-scale demand, much of it illicit or speculative: Silk Road sales totalled 9,519,664 BTC (Feb 2011 to Jul 2013) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road_(marketplace))). BitPay had more than 1,000 merchants by Oct 2012 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bitcoin)). SatoshiDice was large enough to be seen as a "DDoS attack against the Bitcoin network" ([Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Satoshi_Dice)).
- Commodity hardware: until ASICs (Avalon and Butterfly Labs, 2012-2013; Bitmain founded 2013), mining was done on non-specialised hardware. — [Bitcoin Wiki, Avalon](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Avalon); [Bitcoin Wiki, Butterfly Labs](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Butterfly_Labs); [Wikipedia, Bitmain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitmain)
- The binding constraint was banking: Bitstamp left Slovenia for the UK because of "the lack of adequate financial and legal services" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitstamp)). Intersango closed after losing its UK bank ([Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Intersango)). Bitfloor halted after its bank closed its account ([Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitfloor)). Bitcoin Market and TradeHill suffered PayPal and Dwolla fraud ([Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Bitcoin_Market); [Bitcoin Wiki](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/TradeHill)).
- The intermediary layer was where risk, and therefore value, concentrated: "an extensive ecosystem of third-party intermediaries … has emerged", including exchanges, escrow, online wallets, mixers, mining pools and investment services, "be they legitimate or Ponzi schemes". — [Moore, Christin & Szurdi 2018](https://jszurdi.github.io/content/academicpapers/toit18.pdf)

### Inferences
- The decisive and unique condition is the native appreciating asset. It meant users, revenue, financing and the founders' own savings all rose together with the network, and it let teenagers and hobbyists fund themselves from their treasuries or from BTC-denominated share sales. Without it, the other conditions (open code, a forum, software-only products) describe most open-source ecosystems, which did not produce comparable fortunes.
- The near-zero barrier had a cost. It let in anyone, including incompetent and fraudulent operators, which explains the 45-50% failure rate. Low barriers and high failure rates are two faces of the same condition.
- Regulatory absence was a temporary arbitrage, not a durable condition. The survivors were those who converted it into regulatory positioning before enforcement arrived in 2013-2017.

### Gaps
- No academic synthesis of the enabling conditions was consulted in this pass. Candidates: Böhme, Christin, Edelman & Moore, "Bitcoin: Economics, Technology, and Governance", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 2015; Popper, *Digital Gold* (2015). Founder interviews were not consulted.
- GPU-era mining economics (cost per GH/s, 2010-2012) were not sourced.

## 4. Structural comparison with quantum (as of Nov 2026), and better analogies

### Takeaway
The analogy largely breaks on four of Bitcoin's seven conditions: there is no native liquid asset, hardware is capital-intensive and vendor-owned, there are export controls and state ownership from day one, and demand is government/enterprise rather than consumer. It partially holds on open software, cloud access, a global developer community and a hype-driven capital market. It holds strongly only on risk structure: high failure rates, survivorship bias, and value accruing to trust, compliance and intermediation layers. Quantum's equivalent of the "native asset" is listed equity, which funds hardware vendors, not small builders. Better analogies are:
- Cloud/CDN-era deep tech (Akamai, AWS plus API layers like Twilio) for the access and hardware layer.
- Y2K plus SSL/PKI (Verisign, Let's Encrypt) for post-quantum migration, the most "Bitcoin-like" low-capital opening, though it is a classical-cryptography market.
- Deep learning 2012-2017 (Hugging Face, Scale AI) for the open-source tooling and data layer.

- **Where the analogy holds.**
  - (a) High failure base rates: dozens of hardware modalities and many SPAC-era listings are the equivalent of 2011's 40 exchanges, with consolidation already under way and IonQ acting as "acquirer of last resort".
  - (b) Value tends to accrue to trust, custody, compliance and intermediation layers around a core that small players cannot control. The quantum equivalents are access brokering, benchmarking and verification, PQC migration and assurance, and export-compliance services.
  - (c) A speculative capital market runs far ahead of revenue: the Kensho index rose 161% in 12 months against about $1.1B of industry revenue, which echoes the 2011 $31.91 peak and its 93% crash.
  - (d) Late, compliance-first entrants beat first movers.
- **Who captures value early.** In Bitcoin, value went to holders of the asset and to the intermediaries. In quantum it goes to shareholders of hardware vendors, to big tech (IBM, Google), and increasingly to states, which now own equity. A 2026 founder cannot "mine" or "hold" the network's upside the way a 2010 Bitcoiner could.
- **Better analogies, ranked by structural fit.**
  - (1) Cloud/CDN-era deep tech (Akamai 1998-99, AWS 2006 plus API layers like Twilio 2008). Academic spin-outs with heavy infrastructure, access sold as a metered service, and small winners building API and abstraction layers on top of someone else's capital-intensive infrastructure. This matches quantum's cloud-QPU model (IBM, Braket).
  - (2) Y2K plus SSL/PKI for post-quantum cryptography. A compliance- and deadline-driven migration, software and services only, low capital, and buyers who are every enterprise and government agency. The lessons: Verisign's trust position compounded only when tied to a regulated chokepoint (the .com registry); the certificate product itself was sold off in 2010 and commoditised by free automation; and Y2K-type remediation markets end when the deadline passes.
  - (3) Deep learning 2012-2017 for the open-source tooling and data layer. Hugging Face and Scale AI were founded about four years into the wave and compounded through community, data and tooling network effects. The limit is that deep learning ran on commodity GPUs and had immediate consumer-facing uses; quantum has neither.
  - Bitcoin is a good analogy only for the risk profile and for the intermediary layer of PQC/crypto-agility services. It is a poor analogy for the capital, asset and regulatory environment.

## 5. Lessons: timing, which positions compounded, and how long the pre-explosion window lasted

### Takeaway
In Bitcoin the right time to found was not "first" but the second wave, 2-3.5 years after genesis (mid-2011 to 2012). Payoffs took about 9-14 years, and they compounded only for positions in liquidity, custody and trust with licences, merchant distribution, and data-to-wallet user bases. Mining on commodity hardware, pre-order hardware, gambling, standards bodies and anonymous custodial services did not compound. Quantum's pre-explosion window is already 10-27 years old for hardware firms (D-Wave 1999, Rigetti 2013, IonQ 2015). The low-capital openings that most resemble 2011 Bitcoin are in the classical-side migration (PQC) and tooling layers, not in quantum computation itself.

### Inferences
- **Timing rule from Bitcoin.** Founding in the second wave, after the first visible failures and before institutional capital arrives, beat founding first. The window for founding the survivors lasted about 18 months (mid-2011 to end of 2012). Exit or listing came 9-14 years later. A quantum study should therefore ask which second-wave positions open after the first round of quantum consolidation and SPAC failures, rather than "what is first".
- **Positions that compound across all analogies examined.**
  - Trust and custody with regulatory standing (Bitstamp and Coinbase licences; Verisign → .com registry).
  - Liquidity and marketplace network effects (exchanges; Hugging Face as a model hub).
  - Proprietary data or user bases (Blockchain.info; Scale AI's annotation data).
  - Distribution into many end customers (BitPay's merchants; Twilio's developers).
- **Positions that do not compound.** Commodity hardware positions overtaken by specialised capital (CPU/GPU mining to ASICs, and plausibly early quantum component resellers), single-deadline services (Y2K remediation, and plausibly pure PQC inventory consulting after mandated deadlines), and standards bodies without revenue.
- **Most transferable opening for small players in quantum.** It is not computation but the trust and compliance intermediation around the quantum transition: PQC/crypto-agility assurance, quantum-safe certificate and key lifecycle tooling, export-control and deemed-export compliance, benchmarking and verification of vendor claims, and access brokering across clouds. These are software and service positions, closest to the Verisign/Let's Encrypt and Twilio-on-AWS patterns. The Let's Encrypt precedent shows that a compliance product can be commoditised by free automation, so durable value needs a chokepoint: standards, accreditation, data or distribution.
- **Strongest warning.** Bitcoin's early opportunities were funded and rewarded by an appreciating native asset. Quantum has none, so being early in quantum mostly means funding a long pre-revenue window in fiat. That favours VC-scale or state-backed players and makes the near-zero-capital Bitcoin path largely unavailable except in software and services.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of "too early vs on time" across technology waves was consulted (for example, research on first-mover advantage such as Golder & Tellis 1993).
- The fate of Y2K remediation firms after 2000 and the Akamai/Verisign drawdowns in the dot-com crash were not sourced, so the Y2K and CDN "window closed" claims rest only on the deadline logic.
- There was no source for when the deep-learning "pre-explosion window" began (AlexNet 2012) or ended (ChatGPT Nov 2022); these are widely known but were not cited in this pass.
